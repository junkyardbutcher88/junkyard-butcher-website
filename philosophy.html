Why Junkyard. Why Butcher.

The name is two trades stitched together on purpose.

A junkyard is where things go to be judged worthless — until someone who actually knows what they're looking at walks the rows and finds the part that still runs. A butcher doesn't create the animal; the value was already there, marbled through it. The job is knowing exactly where to cut so nothing good gets thrown out with the waste.

Put those together and you get the whole approach: we don't manufacture value out of nothing, and we're not going to sell you on the idea that you need something entirely new. Most of the time, you don't. You need someone willing to go through what you've already built — old posts, an abandoned list, a logo nobody's touched in three years, a customer base nobody's actually talked to — and find the parts worth keeping. Then cut away the rest with intent.

The Belief: Dig Before You Buy

Most marketing spend goes toward acquiring something new — a new logo, a new content pillar, a new platform presence — before anyone has actually audited what's already there. That's backwards, and it's expensive. It's how brands end up with five years of content nobody's revisited, an email list that's never been segmented, and a rebrand every eighteen months because nothing ever gets the chance to compound.

The alternative is a discipline, not a vibe: audit first. Before a single new asset gets made, go through what already exists and grade it honestly. Some of it is scrap. Some of it is the load-bearing wall of the entire brand and nobody noticed. You don't find out which is which without digging — and most agencies skip straight to building because building is what they bill for.

Authenticity Isn't a Filter

The output here is loud, gritty, a little psychedelic, occasionally too dark for a pitch deck — and intentionally, unapologetically not perfect. That's not an aesthetic bolted on after the fact. Polish is easy to fake, and increasingly cheap to fake at scale — anyone can generate a clean, symmetrical, on-brand post in thirty seconds now. What's hard to fake is texture: the rough edge that tells a real person made a real decision.

That's the bet — that in a feed full of interchangeable polish, the thing that actually earns attention is the thing that couldn't have been generated by anyone else's brand. Remarkable, in the literal sense: worth making a remark about. Smoothing that away in the name of "on-brand" consistency is how most brands end up sounding like every other brand in their category.

Audience, Not Algorithm

There's a difference between broadcasting at an audience and building with one, and most brand content still does the former — post, hope the algorithm cooperates, repeat. The community-first approach is slower to set up and faster to compound: let the people already paying attention feel like they're part of the build, not spectators watching a finished product get announced.

Practically, that means meeting people where they already are instead of demanding they come to you — in the inbox, on the phone, in the room at an event — rather than betting everything on one feed's mood that week. A smaller group that actually shows up beats a larger one that scrolls past.

The 7-11-4, In Full

The short version, from the homepage: a buyer needs roughly seven hours of content, across eleven touchpoints, on four different platforms, before they trust a brand enough to hand over money. It's a rule of thumb, not a law of physics — but it's a useful one, because it explains why a single great post rarely converts anything on its own.

In practice, that means planning touchpoints on purpose instead of leaving them to chance: an event recap video that also becomes three social cuts and an email send; an SMS flow that picks up where the inbox left off; a presence across enough different channels that a prospective client runs into the brand more than once before they ever reach out. The frameworks aren't about producing more content — they're about making sure the content that already exists is actually stacking toward something instead of disappearing into a single feed the day it posts.

The 80/20 Hybrid, In Full

Twenty percent of what a brand already does drives eighty percent of its results. That's true of content formats, of email flows, of entire product lines — and almost no one has actually measured which twenty percent it is for them.

So before anything new gets created, the existing library gets audited against real numbers: which recap videos actually got watched to the end, which email sends actually got opened and clicked, which single post quietly outperformed everything else that quarter. That twenty percent gets doubled down on, refined, and repeated on purpose. Everything else gets cut loose — not because it was bad, necessarily, but because attention and budget both compound faster when they're concentrated instead of spread thin trying to maintain everything at once.

Minimal Gear, Maximum Intent

Gear stays minimal by choice. Limitations sharpen a creative decision instead of dulling it — when you can't solve a problem by throwing more equipment at it, you have to actually think about the shot, the sequence, the send. A lot of expensive gear exists to compensate for a lack of a plan.

That said, this isn't a purity test. When a job genuinely calls for the right tool — a specific lens, a specific platform, a specific piece of software — it gets used. No exceptions, no ego about it either way. The constraint is a tool for better decisions, not a personality.

Craft In The Age Of AI

The honest position: tools are fine. Tools have always been fine. What erodes trust isn't the existence of AI in a workflow — it's shipping something nobody actually thought about, dressed up to look considered. That's what "AI slop" actually means here: not the technology, the absence of judgment.

The standard stays the same regardless of what's in the toolkit: does a real decision sit behind this, or did it get templated and pushed out the door because templating is faster? Cut to order, not off the rack — whatever's doing the cutting.

Where This Comes From

Two influences show up constantly in how this business actually runs, not just how it talks about itself.

Chris Do — for treating creative work like an actual business instead of a vanity project. Charging for value instead of hours, understanding the client's real problem before proposing a solution, and taking the business side of "creative" as seriously as the creative side. A lot of talented people undercharge and overdeliver until they burn out; that framing is the antidote.

Seth Godin — for permission marketing, the smallest viable audience, and the idea that "remarkable" means literally worth making a remark about. Godin's whole body of work argues against interrupting strangers and for earning attention from people who actually opted in. The community-over-audience approach described above is that idea applied directly.

Frequently Asked

What does Junkyard Butcher actually do?

Four things, applied together instead of handed off between separate vendors: content creation (video and social, especially event recap), email & SMS marketing, brand identity systems, and brand strategy systems. Most client work has been event recap and social content for cannabis and events brands; one engagement (CVALT) covered the entire marketing and creative department.

What is the 7-11-4 framework?

A rule of thumb for how much trust a buyer needs before they purchase: roughly seven hours of content, across eleven touchpoints, on four different platforms. It's used here to plan content and lifecycle touchpoints on purpose instead of leaving reach to chance.

What is the 80/20 hybrid model?

An application of the Pareto principle to existing brand assets: audit what already exists, identify the roughly twenty percent of content, formats, or flows driving most of the results, and double down there before creating anything net-new.

Does Junkyard Butcher use AI tools?

Where they genuinely help, yes — but direction, editing, and final judgment stay human on every deliverable. The standard isn't "no AI," it's no unconsidered output — nothing ships that wasn't actually thought through.

What kind of brands is this a fit for?

Brands that care about craft and authenticity over churning out volume — a lot of the past work has been cannabis delivery, dispensary, and events brands, but the frameworks and services aren't industry-specific.

How is this different from a typical agency?

One operator working across content, identity, and lifecycle marketing instead of a project getting split across siloed teams who've never talked to each other — and a systems-driven process (the 7-11-4 and 80/20 hybrid) instead of an ad hoc one.
